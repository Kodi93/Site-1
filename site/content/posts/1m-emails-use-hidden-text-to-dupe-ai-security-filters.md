## Details
Artificial intelligence and LLMs can be surprisingly ineffective against text salting, allowing phishing emails to slide right into your inbox. July 16, 2026 Hackers are using simple text salting to evade both static and artificial intelligence-powered email security checks. Since April, researchers at Barracuda Networks observed more than 1 million retail-themed phishing emails that used hidden text to make malicious social engineering look legit to automated security filters. It's a simple age-old trick that's working better today than ever, thanks to an asymmetric advantage afforded by large language models (LLMs). LLMs are helping attackers salt their phishing emails faster and more effectively than ever, whereas the AI-based content analysis engines they're up against don't seem to be putting up much of a defense. "Text salting appears simple, but its continued and growing use shows it remains effective," says Peterson Gutierrez, vice president of information security at Barracuda. "A technique many people associate with older spam-filter evasion can also influence modern AI-based detection." Related: Police Disrupt a €140M Cyber Fraud Ring in Spain The emails that Barracuda researchers have seen employ obvious social engineering tricks, promising rewards, points, and gift cards to targets. They present urgent offers to get people to click on malicious links. In presentation, these emails are even more low-effort. They're not clean or detailed in their brand impersonation and formatting. So if they're such obvious junk, how are they escaping spam filters and reaching people's inboxes? Secure email gateways (SEGs) are the security guards for email inboxes, using whatever rules they know to decide whether to let entrants through. The easy part of the job involves checking for straightforward signals: Is the email sender's domain legitimate, and are they using industry-standard authentication protocols? In this respect, the campaigns observed by Barracuda are mixed but mostly on top of things. They typically use standard DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM) for authentication, and while some sender domains are brand lookalikes, others come from compromised but legitimate websites. The hard part of the SEG's job involves interpreting content: looking for malicious or spammy language by filtering for specific phrases — "XXX" or "Congratulations!" and word usage patterns — salesman-like language, excessive references to money, shady formatting, all caps, and overuse of certain symbols. Outwardly, to the human eye, the emails bypassing email security gateways these days do use language like this. Behind the scenes, though, the SEGs aren't picking it up. Related: Guten Tag, Bonjour, Hola to Our European Cyber Defenders! SEGs don't see the visual presentation of an email, they see the machine-readable data underneath. So if the goal is to evade detection, attackers' efforts would be misplaced trying to make emails look visually legitimate. Instead, they manipulate the HTML code underpinning it. They throw off security gateways by peppering spam content with inoffensive content. This could mean injecting suspicious words like "urgent" or "rewards" with nice words like "puppy" or "book." It could mean inserting random stories or otherwise long passages of unremarkable text to break up spammy paragraphs. Of course, if the human reading the email sees "Urbookgent! Your rewards are puppy expiring" they likely won't read on. The text must be hidden from sight, and there are all kinds of ways to do it. A phisher can shrink the innocuous filler text to a font size of zero, or contain it inside of a viewing window with a height of zero and width of zero. They can overflow the text off-screen , and hide any horizontal scrolling bar so victims don't realize there's more content off to the right. Modern security tools know about text salting and try to un-hide hidden text. In a sense, this works the same way as content filtering: looking for the HTML or CSS rules that make text invisible to users. To account for this, hackers are simply layering multiple salting techniques on top of one another, so that when one fails another might succeed. Related: Cybercriminals Flock to Healthcare Businesses as Attacks Surge Not only do AI security engines perform poorly against salting, AI is also what's making these techniques easier and quicker to produce, as LLMs allow attackers to generate text and modify code at speeds they couldn't otherwise attain. "Defending against this requires more than scanning for suspicious keywords or obvious indicators," Gutierrez says. "Security tools need to evaluate the full context of the message, including the relationship between the visible content, hidden or excessive text, links, sender behavior, and the action the email is trying to prompt."
