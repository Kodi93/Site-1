Security Pro File: On the heels of her recent honors as a Member of the Order of the British Empire (MBE), we take a look back at the events that shaped Jen Ellis' advocacy on behalf of security researchers. July 10, 2026 There comes a point in many storied careers where a single event sits as a professional fulcrum. For Jen Ellis, that moment happened more than a decade ago. The spark didn't come from something that happened to her. Instead, it was what she saw her good friend, HD Moore, go through for doing top-notch security research. She was collaborating with the Metasploit creator, running communications for security firm Rapid7 and the firm's growing body of security research. HD Moore and his team conducted the technical exploration, and she engaged with the broader community and told the world why that research mattered. Everything was humming along until legal threats from a state prosecutor derailed his progress. In 2013, Moore was put through a legal crucible and threatened with potential prosecution for the legitimate security research he was doing through Critical.io, an Internetwide scanning initiative that would eventually become Rapid7's Project Sonar. Related: 'Yellow Teams' Are Defining the Future of AI Security Moore was eventually cleared, but the damage was done. After three months of strain, he questioned whether he wanted to continue doing security research at all. Ellis was incensed. "I got really, really mad about it. Not at HD — I totally understood his point of view. But I was mad that we had ticked every box to say this was legitimate, good-faith research," she says. She dug into the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA), state laws, and more. She didn't like what she saw. The legal and judicial frameworks were acting in opposition to good guys like Moore. Prosecutions were flying, and so were lawsuits from companies that the researchers were trying to help with disclosures. "I found out that researchers all over the place were getting threatened by companies, and I was really indignant about it," she says. "I decided that something needed to be done." It was those big feelings and sense of purpose that spurred her to walk into the office of Rapid7's then-CEO, Corey Thomas, with what seemed like a pretty audacious request. "I want to change the law," she told him, expecting pushback given her total lack of experience in the policy arena. Instead, he told her to give it a try. "I can remember looking at him and being like, 'Are you crazy?'" Ellis says. "I said to him, 'I'm not a policy person. I'm not a lawyer. This seems like a wildly crazy thing to do.'" Thomas came back with a simple question: "But is it the right thing to do?" With her affirmative, he reiterated his green light. After all, he told her at the time, it would probably take up only 5% of her time. Related: As Global Conflicts Go Digital, Businesses Need Wartime Game Plans "It was not 5% of my time," Ellis chuckles. The so-called side project transformed into a career-defining mission that would see Ellis testify before Congress, forge an unlikely partnership with the DoJ, and ultimately help shape how the US government treats security researchers. And as much as the self-effacing Brit would demur at her professional clout, she has made enough of an impact that it recently earned her high praise directly from the Crown. This summer, Ellis was bestowed the Member of the Order of the British Empire (MBE) honors for her contributions to cybersecurity policy. And in true Ellis fashion, her LinkedIn announcement was capped off with a joke: "And to my American friends, the answer to your question is yes, you definitely ARE now required to curtsey whenever you see me. Don't believe anyone that tells you otherwise." This is who Ellis is to the cybersecurity community in a nutshell: Someone who's never afraid to cut up with the best of the jokers while simultaneously doing the serious work of thwarting cybercriminals and keeping white hat hackers from getting swept up in the dragnet. In 2014, Ellis had what she called her first big breakthrough when she made contact with the DoJ's Computer Crimes and Intellectual Property Section (CCIPS). Related: Why Identity Security Is Your Cyber Career Entry Point "I thought that they would be polite and sort of usher me out the door," she admits. "And actually, what happened is they said, 'We completely get it. It's a thing that concerns us. We want to make sure that we are doing the right things to support security. How can we help?'" Among the people she met that day was Leonard Bailey, then working at CCIPS, who would become a key partner in the years ahead. When Ellis invited him to come to Black Hat to meet security researchers, the initial response was predictable: "The Department of Justice does not attend events in Las Vegas." A month later, Bailey called. "Is the offer still open?" he asked her. At Black Hat, Ellis assembled a couple dozen security researchers — including Dan Kaminsky — to meet with Bailey. Her instructions to the researchers were to be respectful but honest. She didn't want them to sugarcoat anything, but she asked that they recognize that Bailey wasn't the enemy. He had, after all, taken the time to come listen to them. Getting their buy-in at that time was a feat, recalls Bailey. This was just a year after the death of Aaron Swartz, who faced CFAA charges for downloading academic articles, which many in the cybersecurity world found unfair. It was a time of peak hacktivism and a higher-than-usual amount of bad blood between security researchers and the feds. The fact that Ellis had built up enough trust and credibility to gather everyone up under that shadow is a testament to what makes her special, Bailey says. "It was a very charged period," he says. " And for her in her capacity to take folks and say, 'We're going to have a respectful meeting with this guy,' was remarkable. There's no way that that meeting or what it produced could have happened without her." The meeting ran hot for about an hour, then something shifted. "We moved on to really what we shared in common," Bailey recalls, "which was an interest in protecting people, protecting networks, protecting victims, and understanding that that's what white hat hackers do."
